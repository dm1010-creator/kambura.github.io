What Makes the Perfect Pour-Over Coffee?

A clear look at how filter-cup design changes extraction, flavour compounds and antioxidant levels — based on real lab results.

The short answer

Among the three ceramic filter cups tested, the new PF2 design produced the highest levels of caffeine, chlorogenic acid, trigonelline, total phenolics and flavonoids, plus strong antioxidant activity. It also gave more stable water flow. PF1 came second. The ordinary commercial V60 (the control) came last on most measures.

Bottom line: A filter cup that holds the paper tightly and spreads water evenly extracts more of the good compounds and gives more consistent results — even when different people pour.

The three filter cups

Researchers compared one everyday commercial cup with two specially designed ceramic cups.

1st – Best

PF2 (new design)

  • One-piece ceramic
  • Eight alternating long & short grooves
  • Holds paper tightly against the wall
  • Even air venting & water flow
2nd

PF1 (earlier design)

  • Nine equal-length grooves
  • Each groove has an inflow + diversion section
  • Better paper fit than a standard V60
3rd – Control

Hario V60

  • Commercial spiral-ridge cone
  • Widely used in cafés and homes
  • The everyday baseline

How they tested it

Everything was kept the same except the filter cup, so differences could be blamed on the cup design.

  1. Beans & grind
    Medium-roasted Arabica beans. Ground in 14 g batches on a flat-blade grinder at three settings: fine, medium and coarse.
  2. Dose & water
    12 g, 14 g, 16 g or 18 g of grounds placed in a paper filter inside the cup. Boiling water poured to a total of 270 g on the scale. Contact time ≈ 2 min 30 s.
  3. Cool & measure
    Coffee cooled to room temperature (25 °C). Then particle size, flow stability, dissolved solids, colour, caffeine, chlorogenic acid, trigonelline, total phenolics (TPC), total flavonoids (TFC) and antioxidant activity (DPPH & ABTS) were measured.

Key results – PF2 came out on top

All differences marked “significant” mean p < 0.05 (statistically unlikely to be chance).

These numbers sit comfortably inside the ranges reported by other filter-coffee studies (caffeine roughly 360–1580 ppm, chlorogenic acid 150–470 ppm).

Why PF2 extracted more of the good stuff

The alternating long and short grooves keep the paper filter pressed evenly against the ceramic wall. That does two useful things:

  • Air can escape uniformly instead of getting trapped.
  • Water spreads outward in a stable pattern instead of finding random channels or leaving dry patches of grounds.

Result: less channelling, less fine-particle clogging at the bottom, more even contact between water and coffee, and therefore higher extraction of caffeine, chlorogenic acids, trigonelline and the phenolic compounds that give antioxidant activity.

The study also noted that water-flow direction with PF2 was more even and the weight of liquid extracted was more consistent — even when different people did the pouring. That stability is valuable both in a café and at home.

What the compounds actually do

Caffeine

The classic stimulant. Also helps scavenge certain free radicals (•OH).

Chlorogenic acids

Major antioxidants in coffee. Linked to the bright, fruity acidity many people like in a good pour-over.

Trigonelline

Breaks down during roasting into compounds that contribute to aroma and perceived sweetness/bitterness balance.

TPC & TFC (total phenolics & flavonoids)

Broad measures of the plant compounds that give coffee its antioxidant capacity. Higher numbers generally mean more of these beneficial molecules made it into the cup.

DPPH & ABTS tests

Two common lab methods that measure how well a liquid can neutralise free radicals. Higher numbers = stronger antioxidant activity in the finished coffee.

Practical tips for a better cup at home

You don’t need the exact PF2 cup to apply the lessons. Focus on the principles that made it win:

1
Paper fit matters
Make sure the filter paper sits snugly against the walls of whatever dripper you use. Large air gaps encourage uneven flow.
2
Pour steadily and centred
The study used a centred stream to a fixed total weight (270 g water for 12–18 g coffee). Consistency beats fancy patterns.
3
Grind size is still king
They tested fine, medium and coarse. Medium is usually the safest starting point for pour-over; adjust if the brew is too slow or too fast.
4
Ratio around 1:15–1:18
14–18 g coffee to 270 g water is roughly that range. Scale up or down as you like, but keep the ratio steady.
5
Freshly roasted Arabica helps
The study used medium-roasted Arabica. Stale beans lose aroma and some of the delicate acids that make a cup lively.

One honest caveat

The lab numbers for extraction yield, bioactive compounds and antioxidant activity clearly favour PF2. Taste and overall enjoyment, however, still need proper consumer sensory tests. The researchers themselves note that “the essential aspect for consumers to taste coffee remains its attractive taste and palatability” and that acceptability of the PF2 coffee should be confirmed in future work.

So: better chemistry and more stable extraction are strong advantages. Whether that always equals “the cup you love most” is something only your own palate can decide.

Quick reference – the takeaway

PF2 > PF1 > standard V60 for extraction efficiency, consistency of flow, and levels of caffeine, chlorogenic acid, trigonelline, phenolics, flavonoids and measured antioxidant activity.

The design feature that mattered most was the fluted wall that keeps the paper filter in close, even contact with the cup — reducing channelling and letting water extract more evenly.
